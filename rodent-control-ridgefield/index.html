Wood stacks and yard habits.

A woodpile against siding gives cover and a path to the wall. Move it several feet from the house and raise it off the ground. Store pet food and seed in lidded bins, and sweep seed that falls from a feeder.

If you see burrows near a foundation, shed or patio, photograph them and call. The backyard and outdoor page explains what a yard visit includes and which changes help most.

Clues worth a call.

These signals call for action, and they help us judge whether the visit can wait for morning.

  • Scrabbling in the ceiling once the house is dark.
  • Droppings in a mudroom, pantry or under a sink.
  • Tooth marks on cartons, cords or door trim.
  • A sharp ammonia smell in a shut room.
  • A mouse or rat seen indoors in daylight.

Where the calendar matters.

A thaw after a long freeze shifts food and shelter around, and rodents follow. Debris from melting snow can hide a fresh burrow, so a walk along the foundation afterward is worth a few minutes.

While you wait: what to do and what to avoid

Agencies broadly agree on how cleanup should go. Their shared steps are collected in short form here.

  • Disturbing dry droppings is what causes trouble, so keep brooms and shop vacuums out of the picture.
  • Ventilation is step one for a musty shed or crawl space: about thirty minutes of open doors.
  • Gloves shield you during the work and soap shields you afterward, so use both.
  • The order is soak, wait, wipe, with roughly five minutes of contact time in the middle.
  • Pull the bag tight and knot it before it goes out, and keep your hands covered whenever a dead rodent has to move.
  • Fever, body aches or breathing trouble in the weeks afterward belong with a physician, who should also hear that rodent waste was nearby. Our trade is pests, not medicine.
