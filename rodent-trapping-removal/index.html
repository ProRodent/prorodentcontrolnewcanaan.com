Why placement matters most.

A trap in the wrong place is just an obstacle. Rodents follow edges, walls and familiar paths, and they avoid open space. A trap set flat against a wall, across a runway, catches animals that a trap in the middle of the room never would.

The technician reads the route first: droppings that show direction, rub marks along a baseboard, gnawing at a corner. Each trap is positioned along that route and its position is noted, so the check visits are quick and nothing is forgotten.

Glue boards are not part of the plan. They catch pets, birds and unintended animals, and they leave a suffering animal for someone to deal with. If you have some at home, take them up before the visit.

How the schedule works.

After setting, we revisit on a timetable chosen for the species. Mice often get cleared quickly, while rats may need several rounds. Each visit empties traps, resets them and records what was caught and where, which shows whether activity is dropping.

  • First check within a few days of setting.
  • Further checks until two consecutive visits find nothing.
  • Traps removed once the sealing work is complete.
  • Notes handed over with the final visit.

Why trapping is half the job.

If animals are removed and gaps stay open, new ones arrive. For this reason a trapping plan always pairs with entry point sealing, which waits until catches slow so nothing is sealed inside a wall.

To see the whole job laid out first, start with an inspection, which maps where to trap and where to seal.

Handling what is caught.

Dead rodents carry germs, so they are handled with gloves or tools and sealed in plastic bags before disposal. If you find a caught animal before we return, avoid touching it with bare hands and read the safety list at the end of this page.

Before you call: handling droppings

Public health agencies give identical advice to every household. Nothing on this list is unique to a visit from us.

  • Soak first and handle later. Dry waste is never swept up.
  • Ventilation is step one for a musty shed or crawl space: about thirty minutes of open doors.
  • At minimum, you need rubber gloves while working and a good scrub when finished.
  • Disinfect before you collect. Wet the waste fully and leave it roughly five minutes.
  • Put waste in a bag, tie it off and drop it with the household trash. Never lift a dead rodent with bare hands.
  • Flu-like illness or breathlessness after exposure is a job for a doctor. Mention the cleanup, and leave health judgments to them.

Trapping questions.

Are the traps safe around pets?

Stations are enclosed and set where pets and children cannot get at them. Mention every animal in the house so placement can work around them.

How many visits does trapping take?

Mice may be cleared in a visit or two. Rats often take longer because they avoid new objects. The plan states the expected number after the first look.

Will you use poison?

Trapping comes first. If a product is ever used, you are told what it is and where it is placed. Loose bait is not left in reach of children or pets.

What happens to the animals?

Animals caught in snap traps are disposed of following the CDC handling steps, with gloves and sealed bags.

Is trapping enough on its own?

No. Without closing the entry points, new animals arrive and the cycle repeats. Sealing is the second half of the job.
