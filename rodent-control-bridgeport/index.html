Why we ask before assuming.

A city of this size has every kind of property, from single houses to multi-unit buildings and businesses. We do not describe particular blocks. We ask whether you share a wall, who handles the waste and whether the building has a basement or a back alley.

Landscaping helps rodents more than owners expect. A limb resting on the roof acts as a bridge, and dense shrubs against the foundation give cover at the wall itself. One slow walk outside shows which plantings matter.

Shared walls, bins and back alleys.

In a building with several units, animals move through shared walls, pipe chases and gaps behind appliances. Sealing in one unit helps only if the neighboring ones are addressed too. Tell the landlord or manager what you found.

Bins and alleys feed rodents on any urban block. Keep lids tight, rinse bins and avoid leaving bags outside overnight. Businesses can read the commercial page for how ongoing visits and notes work.

Across the seasons.

Spring is for nesting. Animals that overwintered inside have their litters in insulation and wall cavities, so a house that seemed still in March can be crowded by May. Moving early also leaves less to clean.

Small clues that add up.

Calls rarely begin with a grand statement. They begin with a sound, a smell or a stray pellet, and the clues below are the usual openers that the walk-through later turns into a plan.

  • Heavy thumping overhead around dusk.
  • Shredded nest material in a vent, engine bay or storage tub.
  • Greasy smears along walls and pipes.
  • A dog or cat fixed on one corner.
  • Droppings that reappear after cleaning.

Before you sweep, spray or bag: pause

Public health agencies give identical advice to every household. Nothing on this list is unique to a visit from us.

  • Disturbing dry droppings is what causes trouble, so keep brooms and shop vacuums out of the picture.
  • Treat thirty minutes of airing as part of the job, not an extra.
  • Germs are invisible, so gloves on, then soap and water when you finish.
  • Saturate the pellets with disinfectant, let them sit five minutes, then take them up with towels.
  • Towels and debris belong in a sealed plastic bag headed for the regular trash, with hands guarded near any carcass.
  • Watch your own health for several weeks. If a fever or breathlessness appears, give your doctor the full story of what you handled.
